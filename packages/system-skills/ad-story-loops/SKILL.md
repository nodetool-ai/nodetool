# Story loops for ads and marketing video

A story loop is one question the viewer wants answered and the payoff that
answers it. This skill produces the **loop map**: which questions the piece
opens, where each one pays off, and what each payoff changes. Write it before
the beat sheet and store it on the board. It decides the order of
information, not the look and not the runtime split.

The board skill still owns the beats and timecodes: `commercial-beat-sheet`
for a spot, `explainer-storyboard` for an explainer, `motion-ad` for a
sound-off feed ad, `ugc-video` for a creator ad, `script-video` when the
voiceover sets the timing. Load the one that fits, plan the loops here, then
fill its beats in the loop map's order.

## The rule

Every question the ad opens earns a meaningful answer, and every answer
changes what the viewer understands about the product or the problem. A
reveal that changes nothing is noise. A question that never pays off is bait,
and the viewer learns not to trust the next one.

## What the evidence supports

Users bring creator frameworks (Kallaway's Story Loops and Dopamine Ladder,
Paterson's TAVIN, open loops, the Pixar Story Spine). Use them as writing
heuristics. None is a validated formula, and "dopamine" is not a measurement
anyone can make on an ad. What controlled research does support:

- Curiosity about a specific, answerable question makes people wait for the
  answer and remember it better (Loewenstein 1994, Kang et al. 2009, Gruber
  et al. 2014).
- Cliffhanger endings raise the stated wish to see the next installment, but
  not enjoyment or suspense (Media Psychology, 2023). Unfinished stories are
  not more memorable: a 2025 meta-analysis found no general recall advantage
  for interrupted tasks.
- Knowing an outcome can raise enjoyment (Leavitt and Christenfeld 2011).
  Viewers value how and why something happens, not only what happens, so a
  piece does not need constant surprise.
- Absorption in a character's situation (narrative transportation) moves
  attitudes. Curiosity alone does not carry an ad.

So: frame claims as questions where it helps, vary the kind and timing of
payoffs, keep the causal chain readable, and close the central question.
Never write "dopamine" or "addictive" into a brief, a report or ad copy as if
it were a result.

## Write the reveals first

1. **List the true reveals.** One to three facts from the brief or the
   product page that would make a viewer say "oh": a number, a before and
   after, a mechanism, a trade-off solved. Each must be a confirmed fact. A
   reveal the brief cannot support is `[CLIENT INPUT NEEDED]`, never invented.
2. **Name the expectation each reveal overturns.** A surprise only lands when
   the viewer already holds the expectation it breaks. "The robot lifts 200
   kg" means little. Show it straining at a small box first, and the jump
   becomes the payoff. The fact stays the same. The setup gives it meaning.
3. **Work backward to the setup.** For each reveal, write the minimum the
   viewer must see or know first: the old way, the cost, the failed attempt,
   the competitor default. That setup is what the early beats show.
4. **Pick the central question.** One question the whole piece answers, in
   the viewer's words: "Can one photo really become a usable ad?" Everything
   else is a nested loop under it.

## The seven stages

Each loop moves through stages 2 to 6. A piece runs stage 1 once, repeats
2 to 6 per loop, and ends on 7. This sequence is a synthesis of the
frameworks above, not a validated model.

| Stage | Job | On screen |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Relevance | Who or what matters, and why to this viewer | The person, the problem or the stake, mid-action |
| 2. Prediction | A clear, answerable question | The setup the reveal will overturn. A line that names the doubt |
| 3. Development | Evidence, a choice, or a complication | The attempt, the mechanism, the obstacle |
| 4. Payoff | The answer, or a real advance on it | The reveal, shown rather than claimed |
| 5. Consequence | What changed and why it matters | The result in use. The trade-off now solved |
| 6. Next question | Open a new loop only when another is warranted | A new doubt the payoff created |
| 7. Satisfaction | Enough closure to earn trust | The central question answered, then the CTA |

Consequence is the stage that separates an ad from a trick: the twist has to
change the viewer's picture of the product. Satisfaction is the stage the
endless-loop model drops. Stop reopening questions and finish.

## Loop budget

Starting points for feed and pre-roll ads, not research results. Adjust to
the platform and the product.

| Runtime | Loops | First payoff by |
|---|---|---|
| 6–15s | The central question only | ~55% of runtime |
| 30s | Central question plus one nested loop | ~45% |
| 60s | Central question plus two nested loops | ~40% |
| 90s and longer | Central question plus up to four, each closed before the next opens | ~30% |

A payoff held too long frustrates rather than holds. Never have more than two
loops open at once, and never open a loop in the last 15% of a piece unless it
is a series hook (below).

## Mapping onto the board skills

Fill the board skill's beats in the loop map's order. Typical fits:

| Board skill | Relevance + question | Development | Payoff + consequence | Satisfaction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `commercial-beat-sheet` | Hook, brand landing | Problem | Solution/USP, proof | CTA, reiteration, closing echo of the hook |
| `explainer-storyboard` | Problem, stakes | Shift | Mechanism beats, the aha, outcome/proof | CTA, sign-off |
| `motion-ad` | Hook headline over the product in context | Before state, the tap | Before becomes after, proof line | End card |
| `ugc-video` | Hook to camera | The thing happening physically | The result on the creator | Verdict to camera |

`commercial-beat-sheet`'s "close every loop you open" and its hook archetypes
still apply. Choose the archetype that sets up the strongest reveal: Result
First and Cost Reveal open a "how?" loop, In Media Res Failure and Cold
Contradiction open a "what went wrong?" loop.

## Hooks that open a loop, and hooks that only bait

A loop-opening hook names a specific doubt the viewer can hold: "We tried to
make a full product ad from one photo without editing a frame." The viewer
knows the challenge and can predict an outcome.

Bait withholds the subject itself: "You won't believe what happened", a
blurred product, a "wait for it" with no stake. It raises a question the
viewer has no reason to care about, and the payoff cannot change their
understanding of anything.

A mid-piece rehook ("it looked professional, until we noticed one detail")
works only when the detail pays off within a few seconds and matters to the
buyer. Do not script a reversal the product's real story does not contain.

## People, not only questions

Product facts alone rarely move anyone. Give the viewer someone whose goal
they understand, the creator, the customer, the team, and show what the
product changes for them. Keep two things apart when writing:

- **Character commitment**: how much the person on screen wants the outcome.
- **Audience investment**: whether the viewer cares. It comes from
  recognising the problem as theirs and seeing what is at stake.

A large on-screen sacrifice does nothing if the viewer does not know why it
matters. Put the stake in stage 1, in the viewer's terms.

## Series and episodic content

For a run of marketing videos (a launch week, a build-in-public series, a
how-we-made-it set), each episode closes its own central question, then
opens the next episode's question in the last beat. Cliffhangers raise intent
to watch the next one. They do not make this one better, and an ad that ends
unresolved leaves the buyer without the answer the CTA depends on. Use an
open ending only when a next episode exists and is scheduled, and still pay
off the episode's own question first.

Across episodes, repeat what the viewer can rely on: the same presenter, the
same format, answers that arrive. Loyalty to a creator or brand comes from
consistent value, expertise and trust, not from escalating suspense.

## Store the loop map on the board

The loop map lives on the storyboard so a later pass, or a variant, can read
it:

- Put the whole map in the board `brief` with `create_storyboard` or
  `edit_storyboard` `set_board {brief}`, under a `LOOP MAP` heading.
- Tag each shot's loop job in its `notes` field (`add_shot` or `update_shot`):
  `L1 open: can one photo make a usable ad?`, `L1 payoff`, `L1 consequence`,
  `central close`. `notes` never enters a render prompt, so it costs nothing
  in the picture.

```text
LOOP MAP
CENTRAL: <the question the whole piece answers, in the viewer's words>
REVEALS: <1–3 confirmed facts, each with its source>
L1  expectation: <what the viewer assumes>
    question:    <the doubt the shot names>
    opens:       <timecode, shot slug>
    payoff:      <timecode, shot slug, what is shown>
    consequence: <what the viewer now understands differently>
L2 ...
CLOSE: <timecode, shot slug, how the central question is answered>
OPEN AT END: <none, or the next episode's question>
```

The shot text the renderer reads (`action`, `motion`) still follows the
chosen model's prompting skill. The loop map changes what each shot shows and
in which order, not how the prompt is worded.

## Review the cut against the map

After assembly, check each loop on the real frames:
`preview_timeline_frame {timeline_id, times_ms, sheet: true}` at every
question and payoff timecode in the map, then `view_image` on the sheet.

- Is the expectation visible before the reveal? A reveal with no setup on
  screen is a non-sequitur.
- Does each payoff arrive by its planned timecode, and is it shown rather
  than claimed in a super?
- Is the consequence on screen, the product in use after the reveal?
- Does the question read muted? On a sound-off feed ad the question is a
  headline (`caption-titles`), not only voiceover.
- Is the central question answered before the CTA, and is nothing left open
  that the brief did not ask to leave open?

Name each failure against its loop (`L2 payoff has no setup`), fix the
board or the cut, and preview the same timecodes again.

## Worked example: a 45-second product demo

The central question: can one product photo become a usable, editable ad?

| Time | Stage | Shot |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5s | Relevance, L1 question | "We tried making a full product ad from one photo, without editing a frame." The photo on screen |
| 5–13s | Development, L2 opened | Generation starts. "It looked surprisingly professional, until we noticed one detail." |
| 13–22s | L2 payoff | The generated ad has changed the product's logo. It looks good and is commercially unusable |
| 22–34s | Consequence, L3 question | "So we kept the product untouched and generated only the motion graphics around it." The product stays fixed while the graphics move |
| 34–45s | L1 and L3 payoff, satisfaction | The finished ad beside its editable timeline. The question is answered, the trade-off shown, then the CTA |

Each reveal changes the viewer's understanding of the problem: the obvious
method fails for a reason a buyer cares about, and the fix is the product's
real mechanism.

## When the piece is done

`ad-creative-testing` designs the matched variants that show whether a loop
structure actually beats a direct version for this product and audience.
Retention alone is not the result. A cut people finish but do not understand
or act on has entertained the wrong audience.
